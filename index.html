thoughtfeel

An extra sense for concepts

Some people have it and some don’t. It’s like the mind’s eye: some people can picture things vividly, and some, with aphantasia, see nothing at all. If you have thoughtfeel, you can imagine in concepts, not just words or images.

This is a practical guide to harnessing that ability to learn faster and remember more.

BY TED SHACHTMAN · WATCH THE VIDEOS

The 1-minute test

Do you have it?

Imagine one thing in a few ways. There are no wrong answers.

  1. Imagine walking into a surprise party your friends secretly planned for you. Can you picture it?
  2. Now hear it: everyone shouting “Surprise!”, or the words in your head. Can you hear it?
  3. Let the picture and the sounds go. You still know what you were imagining. That’s the feeling of knowing, and it isn’t thoughtfeel. Imagining uses a sense in your mind, like picturing or hearing. Knowing doesn’t.
  4. Now imagine the party in concepts, with no picture and no words. Pay attention to the concepts you’re imagining, and try noticing detail in them. Does noticing detail in the concepts feel like something, the way noticing the exact color of something you picture does?

Want to be sure? Take the 4-minute quiz.

The sense

What is thoughtfeel?

thoughtfeel is a sense for the meaning of your thoughts, the way vision is a sense for light and hearing is a sense for sound.

Like vision and hearing, it differs from person to person. For some people, taking in and imagining concepts is the default way they experience the world. For others, it’s much quieter. Just as people can imagine in pictures or in sounds, people with thoughtfeel can imagine in concepts.

For people with thoughtfeel, the meaning of every thought has a particular feel, almost like a taste, and that feel is specific enough to recognize what they’re thinking from the feel alone.

The feel alone is enough to tell what the thought is

Suppose you handed someone with thoughtfeel only the sensation of a thought, without telling them what it was about. They could attend to that sensation and tell you it’s an animal, and specifically that it’s a giraffe.

It’s the same way you can look at an object in front of you, recognize it and say what it is. The feel doesn’t just tell them a thought is there. It tells them what the thought is.

Remembering a line word for word

People with thoughtfeel can remember something by storing it in this sense. Take a line from Jon Bellion’s “New York Soul”:

Bring me down to Brooklyn if I lose my life.

The sensation of that thought is specific enough to pinpoint exactly what the line is. It tells someone with thoughtfeel that the word is bring, and that the line is spoken to someone, asking them to bring the singer down to Brooklyn. It isn’t carry: “Carry me down to Brooklyn if I lose my life” feels distinctly different. The rest of the line is just as specific.

The whole line is stored as one big sensation. It’s like a painting: you can take it in as one object, then look at its different parts and notice things about each of them.

This isn’t limited to lyrics. Any sentence can be memorized this way, including something a person said. The whole meaning of the sentence is stored as one felt thought, and if that feel is specific enough, the exact wording can be recalled from it. Held this way, it tends to stick around for a long time.

Four ways to hold the same line

  • In soundsHear the words in your inner voice.
  • In imagesPicture the words on a page, or a scene that stands for them.
  • KnowingKnow what the line is about, without the exact words.
  • In conceptsImagine the line’s meaning in concepts. With thoughtfeel, the exact words are in it. This is the one the lessons teach.
  • Not your mind’s eye or inner voice. Pictures and words can come along with a thought, but the feel is there without them. “Taste” is an analogy.
  • Not an emotion. For people with thoughtfeel, 72 and 86 feel different from each other, but neither one feels happy or sad.
  • Not the feeling of knowing. Everyone knows what they’re thinking. thoughtfeel gives the thought itself detail you can notice, the way a picture has detail.

Three words you’ll hear in the videos A texture is one part of a thought’s feel, like the animal in the thought of a giraffe. The fabric is the whole felt thought. Weaving is combining textures to form a new thought.

The lessons

What is it useful for?

thoughtfeel is a sense, like your mind’s eye or your inner voice. Those senses come with well-known techniques you can learn to remember and learn better. The memory palace uses your mind’s eye to pull off remarkable feats of memory, like memorizing a 1,000-digit number in an hour. Your inner voice remembers more when you put the words to a song, which is why the alphabet song works.

thoughtfeel has techniques of its own, and learning them can improve how you think and learn. Each lesson below teaches one.

Use it · about 5 minutes

Test if you can use it to think

If you can imagine in concepts, see whether you can think with it. This exercise has you hold five everyday words in order by imagining them in concepts, then checks what you kept.

  1. Bring the first word, car, to mind and imagine it in concepts: attend to what a car feels like as an idea, not to a picture of one.
  2. Give it its position. Say aloud “Car is first,” attending to car as you say car and giving it that place as you say first. If “first” feels vague, think of the first person in a queue and the first song on an album, and attend to what they share.
  3. Do the same for the other four words, giving each one its position.
  4. Go back through the list. Aim for each word’s position to come with it, so that the feel of a word also carries its place.

Talking aloud helps direct what you are doing in thoughtfeel.
